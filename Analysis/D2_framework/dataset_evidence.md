- [D02] (EPFL) GenAI capability, single attempt (CS): With a single zero-shot prompt, GPT-4 answered 46.7% of Computer Science questions fully correctly and 56.6% at least mostly correctly. GPT-3.5 reached 39.6% and 48.0%. | Limitations: Models are GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 (2023); current models are likely stronger, but this dataset cannot show that. Measures AI capability, not student learning. Open answers were graded automatically by GPT-4, not by humans.
- [D03] (EPFL) GenAI capability, several attempts (CS): If a student tries all 8 prompting strategies and can pick the best answer, GPT-4 produced a fully correct answer for 85.6% of CS questions (92.1% at least mostly correct). Only 14.4% of CS questions were never fully answered by any prompt. Under a majority rule (>= 5 of 8 prompts correct) the figure is 56.3%. | Limitations: Models are GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 (2023); current models are likely stronger, but this dataset cannot show that. Measures AI capability, not student learning. Best of 8 overstates what a typical student would get, because it assumes they can pick the correct answer from several.
- [D04] (EPFL) MCQ versus chance: On single-answer MCQs (median 4 options), GPT-4 zero-shot was correct on 52.2%, compared with an expected 26.5% from random guessing. Best of 8 reached 88.6%. | Limitations: Models are GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 (2023); current models are likely stronger, but this dataset cannot show that. Measures AI capability, not student learning. Multiple-answer MCQs excluded from this calculation.
- [D05] (EPFL) MCQ versus open answer (CS): For GPT-4 in CS courses, 50.8% of MCQs were answered correctly zero-shot. For open answers, 38.4% were fully correct but 68.2% were at least mostly correct. Mean grades did not differ clearly (Mann-Whitney p = 0.61). | Limitations: Models are GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 (2023); current models are likely stronger, but this dataset cannot show that. Measures AI capability, not student learning. MCQ is exact-match marked while open answers were marked by GPT-4 on a 4-point scale, so the two are not directly comparable.
- [D06] (EPFL) Model generation: GPT-4 scored 13.3 percentage points higher than GPT-3.5 on mean grade and did better on 63.7% of questions (worse on 17.4%). | Limitations: Models are GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 (2023); current models are likely stronger, but this dataset cannot show that. Measures AI capability, not student learning.
- [D07] (EPFL) Prompting strategy: For GPT-4 on MCQs, prompting strategy changed the mean grade by up to 15 points (lowest Zero-Shot 0.50, highest CoT (Four-Shot) 0.65). For open answers the range was only 6 points. | Limitations: Models are GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 (2023); current models are likely stronger, but this dataset cannot show that. Measures AI capability, not student learning. Statistical significance mainly reflects the large sample; focus on the size of the range.
- [D08] (EPFL) Course-level variation (CS): GPT-4 zero-shot mean grade was 0.5 or higher in 13 of 17 CS courses (all 17 with best of 8). Course means ranged from 0.36 to 0.81. | Limitations: Models are GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 (2023); current models are likely stronger, but this dataset cannot show that. Measures AI capability, not student learning. Course question sets were not complete exams and do not reflect actual course weighting.
- [D09] (EPFL) Course level (all programs): GPT-4 did not perform worse on Master's-level questions (mean 0.646) than on Bachelor's-level questions (0.622). | Limitations: Models are GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 (2023); current models are likely stronger, but this dataset cannot show that. Measures AI capability, not student learning. Program mix differs between levels.
- [D11] (EPFL) Question characteristics (exploratory): GPT-4 scored lower on open-answer questions containing code (0.61, n = 1,212) than on those without (0.69, n = 864). Longer MCQs were harder: shortest quartile 0.64 vs longest 0.51. Overall correlation between question length and grade was weak (rho = -0.12). | Limitations: Models are GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 (2023); current models are likely stronger, but this dataset cannot show that. Measures AI capability, not student learning. Heuristics are rough and unvalidated; differences are modest. Treat as exploratory.
- [D12] (Mircea) Prevalence of GenAI use: 92.7% (139/150) of capstone students reported using GenAI in their project, and 38.7% (58/150) used it often or continuously. | Limitations: Self-reported survey from one German university's software engineering capstone; voluntary response, so likely selection bias. Does not measure understanding or performance.
- [D13] (Mircea) Purposes of use: The most common uses were generating or debugging code 64.7% (97/150) and learning 64.0% (96/150), followed by documentation 48.0% (72/150). Few used it for high-level design 18.0% (27/150) or requirements 16.7% (25/150). | Limitations: Self-reported survey from one German university's software engineering capstone; voluntary response, so likely selection bias. Does not measure understanding or performance. Categories were fixed by the questionnaire.
- [D14] (Mircea) Tools: General chatbots were used by 74.7%, integrated coding tools (e.g. Copilot, Cursor) by 46.0%, and the university-hosted instance by only 5.3%. | Limitations: Self-reported survey from one German university's software engineering capstone; voluntary response, so likely selection bias. Does not measure understanding or performance. Local context: the university tool may have been new or little promoted.
- [D15] (Mircea) Interaction modes: 34.0% (51/150) frequently let GenAI propose solutions that they then reviewed, and 37.3% (56/150) frequently gave their own solution to GenAI to review. The two modes were used about equally (Wilcoxon p = 0.20); 62 of 150 rated them the same and only 8 relied heavily on AI-first while rarely using student-first. | Limitations: Self-reported survey from one German university's software engineering capstone; voluntary response, so likely selection bias. Does not measure understanding or performance. Two single items; no observation of actual behaviour.
- [D16] (Mircea) Heavier use and AI-first reliance: Overall use frequency was more strongly associated with AI-first use (rho = 0.77) than with student-first use (rho = 0.56). Both are significant after Holm correction. | Limitations: Self-reported survey from one German university's software engineering capstone; voluntary response, so likely selection bias. Does not measure understanding or performance. Correlation only; overlapping item wording may inflate the association.
- [D17] (Mircea) Self-rated skill: Self-rated programming skill was not significantly associated with use frequency (rho = -0.17, Holm p = 0.16). Code generation was reported by 69.2% of low-, 68.5% of medium- and 48.3% of high-skill students, but the difference was not significant (chi-square p = 0.12). | Limitations: Self-reported survey from one German university's software engineering capstone; voluntary response, so likely selection bias. Does not measure understanding or performance. Only 13 students rated themselves low skill, so the test has little power.
- [D18] (Mircea) Perceived usefulness: 64.0% (96/150) rated student GenAI use in the project as useful and 12.7% (19/150) as harmful. | Limitations: Self-reported survey from one German university's software engineering capstone; voluntary response, so likely selection bias. Does not measure understanding or performance.
- [D19] (Mircea) Students' own advice (coded): In free-text advice to future students, the most frequent directive was 'Retain Understanding' (39.3%, 35/89), then 'Reviewing Outputs' (29.2%) and 'Appropriate Degree Of Usage' (24.7%). | Limitations: Self-reported survey from one German university's software engineering capstone; voluntary response, so likely selection bias. Does not measure understanding or performance. Only 89 of 150 students answered; coding was done by the original authors and its reliability is not reported in the files.
- [D20] (Mircea) Client perspective: The most common client concern was students' lack of independent understanding in client meetings (7 of 11). Quality, privacy and traceability were each named by 4 of 11. Yet 7 of 11 clients wanted students actively supported and encouraged to use GenAI. | Limitations: Self-reported survey from one German university's software engineering capstone; voluntary response, so likely selection bias. Does not measure understanding or performance. Very small sample (11); report as counts, not percentages. The 11 are individuals, not 11 independent organisations (several stakeholders per project). The questionnaire opened by telling clients that over 90% of students used GenAI and that a ban or controls would be neither feasible nor sensible, so support for encouraging use may be inflated; state this whenever these figures are cited.
- [D22] (StudyChat) What students asked the AI for: The average student used 47.1% of prompts for understanding (conceptual or contextual questions) and 31.2% to ask the AI to write or edit (write code alone 15.7%). Only 16.7% of students used write-or-edit requests for half or more of their prompts. Verification requests were rare (3.7%). | Limitations: One AI course (COMPSCI 383) at one US university, 2024-25, with a course chatbot (gpt-4o-mini); students opted in to sharing chats and grades, so likely selection bias. Dialogue-act labels were generated by GPT-4.1, not human-coded. Observational, so associations are not causal. Use of other AI tools outside the course chatbot is not captured. Label groups are a rough proxy for 'asking for the solution'.
- [D23] (StudyChat) First move in a chat: 29.0% of chats opened with a writing request (313 of 2,214 with 'write code'), and 28.1% opened with a conceptual question. | Limitations: One AI course (COMPSCI 383) at one US university, 2024-25, with a course chatbot (gpt-4o-mini); students opted in to sharing chats and grades, so likely selection bias. Dialogue-act labels were generated by GPT-4.1, not human-coded. Observational, so associations are not causal. Use of other AI tools outside the course chatbot is not captured. Chat-level count, so heavy users contribute more chats.
- [D24] (StudyChat) AI-permitted assignments vs proctored exams: Students averaged 94.8% on take-home programming assignments where the course chatbot was allowed, and 86.3% on proctored exams without AI. 90.6% scored higher on assignments; the median gap was 7.7 points (95% CI 6.4 to 9.3). The gap appeared in both semesters. | Limitations: One AI course (COMPSCI 383) at one US university, 2024-25, with a course chatbot (gpt-4o-mini); students opted in to sharing chats and grades, so likely selection bias. Dialogue-act labels were generated by GPT-4.1, not human-coded. Observational, so associations are not causal. Use of other AI tools outside the course chatbot is not captured. Assignments and exams differ in content, format and difficulty, so the gap is not caused by AI alone. No pre-GenAI comparison cohort. Exam 2 carried extra credit (41 of 70 Fall 2024 and 15 of 111 Spring 2025 students scored above 100%), which raises exam averages and narrows the gap.
- [D25] (StudyChat) Assignment marks bunch at the top: 91.7% of students averaged 90% or more on assignments, compared with 39.8% on exams. Assignment scores spread less (SD 6.0 vs 9.0 points) and agreed only moderately with exam scores (rho = 0.53 pooled; 0.68 in Fall 2024 and 0.44 in Spring 2025). | Limitations: One AI course (COMPSCI 383) at one US university, 2024-25, with a course chatbot (gpt-4o-mini); students opted in to sharing chats and grades, so likely selection bias. Dialogue-act labels were generated by GPT-4.1, not human-coded. Observational, so associations are not causal. Use of other AI tools outside the course chatbot is not captured. The ceiling may also reflect generous marking or autograders, not only AI use. Exam 2 carried extra credit (41 of 70 Fall 2024 and 15 of 111 Spring 2025 students scored above 100%), which raises exam averages and narrows the gap.
- [D26] (StudyChat; logs from an optional course chatbot, never assessed or graded) How students used the AI vs grades: No usage measure (number of prompts, write-or-edit share, write-code share, understanding share, verification share) was clearly associated with assignment marks, exam marks or the gap: All 45 correlations had Holm-adjusted p >= 0.89, and the largest was rho = 0.20. Exam averages were 85.4%, 86.7% and 88.1% for the low, middle and high write-or-edit tertiles (Kruskal-Wallis p = 0.36). | Limitations: One AI course (COMPSCI 383) at one US university, 2024-25, with a course chatbot (gpt-4o-mini); students opted in to sharing chats and grades, so likely selection bias. Dialogue-act labels were generated by GPT-4.1, not human-coded. Observational, so associations are not causal. Use of other AI tools outside the course chatbot is not captured. A null result is not proof of no effect; shares are averaged across all assignments.
- [D27] (StudyChat) Using the AI on an assignment vs its mark: For each assignment, students who used the chatbot on it did not score clearly differently from those who did not (0 of 14 assignment-by-semester comparisons significant after Holm adjustment). Most marks were already near full. | Limitations: One AI course (COMPSCI 383) at one US university, 2024-25, with a course chatbot (gpt-4o-mini); students opted in to sharing chats and grades, so likely selection bias. Dialogue-act labels were generated by GPT-4.1, not human-coded. Observational, so associations are not causal. Use of other AI tools outside the course chatbot is not captured. Non-users may have used other AI tools; 'not used' means only 'did not use the course chatbot'.
- [D28] (StudyChat) Variation by assignment: The share of prompts asking for code ranged from 8.3% on a6 to 25.3% on a3, while the understanding share stayed between 45.2% and 53.2%. | Limitations: One AI course (COMPSCI 383) at one US university, 2024-25, with a course chatbot (gpt-4o-mini); students opted in to sharing chats and grades, so likely selection bias. Dialogue-act labels were generated by GPT-4.1, not human-coded. Observational, so associations are not causal. Use of other AI tools outside the course chatbot is not captured. Assignment content was not coded, so we cannot say which task features drive the difference.

- [J1] (StudyChat, Jiajun) Among students who used the chatbot, on average 14.5% of new code lines in submissions match the student's own chatbot replies verbatim (23.2% of submissions have >=25%; 11.1% of 8-word prose shingles vs 0.3% control). Within an assignment, going from 0% to 100% overlap changed the assignment score by -1.0 points (95% CI -4.3 to +2.3, n=518): marking did not respond to AI-authored content.
- [J2] (StudyChat, Jiajun) Production delegation and verbatim reuse were highest in a scaffolded tutorial notebook (a3) and lowest in applied scenarios requiring explanations/reports (a2, a6); descriptive only (12 units).
- [J3] (EPFL, Jiajun) Under a best-of-8 adversary the open-answer format keeps no mean advantage over MCQ (0.904 vs 0.903, CS). Item-format features explain little of AI resistance (pseudo R2 0.12-0.15) while between-course resistance ranges 4.3%-47.4%; the apparent benefit of longer answers is largely an incompleteness artefact cheaply fixed by re-prompting. Security does not reside in question format.
- [J4] (Hannover course design as reported in Mircea et al. 2026; literature, not a dataset finding) The course's own assessment adds a rotating 5-15 minute one-to-one tutor conversation on artefacts and commit history to verify individual contributions (about one tutor per three teams of 9-10). Evidence of feasibility, not of effectiveness.
- [J5] (Hannover/Mircea, Jiajun) The client questionnaire told clients that >90% of students used GenAI and that bans/controls were not feasible before asking for views; client support figures may be inflated; the 11 clients are individuals, not 11 organisations.
- [J6] (StudyChat, Jiajun) The source paper's claims that dialogue-act features predict grades largely fail stricter testing (gain in R2 no larger than noise; 0-1 of 674 coefficients survive FDR); logged AI-use behaviour does not identify which students have larger assignment-exam gaps.
